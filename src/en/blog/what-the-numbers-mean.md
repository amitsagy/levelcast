There are four numbers on this site, and they all come from the same minute. It's a real minute of a Hebrew podcast, with a host and a guest, that went through the same code that runs in the app. It's worth knowing what each one measures, because without the explanation they're easy to read the wrong way round.

## The first two: the gap

The gap between speakers is the difference between the quiet parts of the minute and the loud parts.

In the original version the gap is <span dir="ltr">7.1 dB</span>. After levelling it's <span dir="ltr">2.9 dB</span>.

A decibel is a relative scale, not an ordinary one-two-three scale. It's built so that small differences in the number are very noticeable differences to the ear. That's why the drop from <span dir="ltr">7.1</span> to <span dir="ltr">2.9</span> sounds much bigger than it looks on paper. The first gap is the kind that sends your hand to the knob. The second is a difference the ear accepts as a natural part of a conversation: two people, two voices, but in the same room.

## The other two: the overall level

Here a different unit comes in, dBFS. It measures level relative to the maximum a digital file can hold. Zero is the ceiling, so all the values are negative: the closer the number is to zero, the louder the sound.

In the original version the overall level of the minute is <span dir="ltr">-20.7 dBFS</span>. After levelling it's <span dir="ltr">-18.1 dBFS</span>. So after levelling, the minute is louder, not quieter.

## Why the second number matters

There's a very cheap way to close a gap between two voices: turn the loud one down until it matches the quiet one. The gap disappears and the gap numbers look great. But now the whole episode is quiet, and in the car, over the road noise, you have to turn it up. You're back at the knob, just from the other direction.

That's why the overall level sits next to the gap. It shows the gap was closed from the right direction: the quiet voice came up, the loud one was held back, and the episode as a whole stayed at a level you can hear over the road.

## What the numbers don't say

They describe one minute. In another episode, with other speakers and another recording, the gap before will be different, and so will the result. In an episode where both speakers already talk at the same level, there isn't much to even out, and the levelling will hardly be noticeable. That's fine, and it's what should happen.

What the minute does show is that the method works on a real recording, with no manual editing, while it plays.
