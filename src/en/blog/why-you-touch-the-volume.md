In every podcast with two people there's a moment when your hand leaves the wheel. The host talks loudly, the guest answers quietly, you turn it up, and then the host comes back and it's shouting.

It happens so often you stop noticing. Your hand just goes to the knob, like it goes to the indicator.

## It isn't your ears or your speakers

The first thought is that something's wrong with the equipment: the car speaker, the Bluetooth, the phone. But play the same episode at home on good headphones and the gap is still there. It's just less annoying, because at home there's no road noise swallowing the quiet voice.

The gap is inside the file itself. It was created long before the episode reached your phone.

## Every voice was recorded differently

The host sits in their own studio, close to a microphone they know, at a distance they've already found. The guest sometimes comes to the same studio but sits further back, or talks in another direction. And sometimes they aren't there at all: they're on a remote call, on a laptop microphone, in a room with an echo.

The result is two channels that each sound fine on their own, and that, once joined into one conversation, have one voice much louder than the other.

## Why editing doesn't close it

In a lot of productions, editing takes care of the episode's level as a whole: it shouldn't be too quiet compared with other episodes, and it shouldn't have sharp jumps. That's important work, and it works. But it looks at the episode as one unit.

The gap inside the conversation, between two people talking one after the other, is a different problem. Closing it means handling each voice separately, through the whole episode, every time the speaker changes. Not every production gets to that, and certainly not in every episode.

## What to do about it

You can keep your hand near the knob. Or you can hand the job to the player.

LevelCast listens to the episode as it plays and evens out the speakers as it goes. Whoever speaks quietly comes up, whoever is loud is held back. The processing runs on the iPhone, and the audio comes straight from the producer's server without passing through anyone.

To check that it works, a real minute of a Hebrew podcast went through the same code that runs in the app. The gap between speakers dropped from <span dir="ltr">7.1 dB</span> to <span dir="ltr">2.9 dB</span>. The overall level actually went up, from <span dir="ltr">-20.7 dBFS</span> to <span dir="ltr">-18.1 dBFS</span>. In other words, levelling doesn't solve the problem by turning everything down. It brings the speakers closer together.
