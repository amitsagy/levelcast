In a lot of podcasts with two people, there's a moment when one hand leaves the wheel. The host talks loudly, the guest answers quietly, so you turn it up. Then the host blasts back through the speakers.

It happens so often that you stop noticing. Your hand goes to the knob on its own, the way it goes to the indicator.

## Maybe it's the speaker?

The first thought is that something's wrong with the equipment: the car speaker, the Bluetooth, the phone. But play the same episode at home on good headphones and the gap is still there. It just bothers you less, because at home there's no road noise swallowing the quiet voice. In the car, any difference between the voices stands out more.

The gap is in the file itself, and it was there long before the episode reached your phone.

## Every voice was recorded differently

The host sits in their own studio, close to a microphone they know, at a distance they've long since worked out. Even when the guest comes to the same studio, they sometimes sit further back or talk in a different direction. And sometimes they join over a remote call instead, on a laptop microphone, from a room with an echo.

Each of those two channels sounds fine when you listen to it alone. Put them together into one conversation and one of them is simply much louder than the other.

## Why editing doesn't close it

In a lot of productions, editing takes care of the level of the episode as a whole. It makes sure the episode isn't too quiet compared with others, and that there are no sharp jumps in it. But it treats the episode as one unit.

The gap between two people taking turns to talk is a different problem. Closing it means handling each voice separately, through the whole episode, every time the speaker changes. Not every production gets to that step, and certainly not in every episode.

## So what do you do

You can keep driving with your hand near the knob. Or you can hand that job to the player.

LevelCast listens to the episode as it plays and evens out the speakers as it goes, so the quiet voice comes up and the loud one is held back. The processing runs on the iPhone, and the audio comes straight from the producer's server without passing through anyone in the middle. There are three levelling strengths, off, normal and strong, and you can switch between them from the player.

To check that this actually works, a real minute of a Hebrew podcast went through the same code that runs in the app. The gap between speakers dropped from <span dir="ltr">7.1 dB</span> to <span dir="ltr">2.9 dB</span>. The overall level actually went up. Before levelling it was <span dir="ltr">-20.7 dBFS</span>, and after it <span dir="ltr">-18.1 dBFS</span>. So the gap closed without the whole episode being turned down, and the two voices simply moved closer together.
